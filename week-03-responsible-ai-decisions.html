Responsible AI is a decision habit.

Today we practice what is allowed, what needs permission, and what should not be done. The goal is not to memorize every policy. The goal is to pause before using AI in a way that affects people, data, sources, or authorship.

8-15 min

Use five questions before using AI.

This simplified model is informed by risk-management thinking: map the task, identify who could be affected, measure what needs evidence, then manage the action with review.

What is the task?

Name the real job: brainstorm, edit, summarize, generate, rank, decide, cite, code, or submit. Different jobs carry different responsibilities.

Example: "Use AI to brainstorm three possible project topics" is different from "Use AI to write my final reflection."
Pacing: 7 minutes. Keep this as a reusable checklist. Students will apply it repeatedly in scenarios.
Decision labels

Classify the action, then explain why.

Usually allowed if policy permits

Appropriate support when the student remains responsible, checks the output, and discloses use if required.

Ask first or revise

The idea might be acceptable, but data, source material, tool choice, or assignment rules need clarification.

Not allowed

The action misrepresents authorship, exposes private data, invents evidence, or uses AI for an unauthorized decision.

allowed is conditional: policy + permission + disclosure + verification

Privacy default

Do not paste identifiable private or peer data into AI by default.

Red examples

  • A classmate's draft with their name.
  • A screenshot of grades or feedback.
  • A personal email, medical detail, or family issue.
  • A private document from work or an internship.

Safer alternatives

  • Use invented or anonymized examples.
  • Ask the instructor which tool is approved.
  • Describe the problem without identifiers.
  • Get permission before using someone else's content.
private data + no permission + third-party AI = stop

Micro-lab

Claim calibration: say how you know.

When an AI gives an answer, identify what kind of claim each sentence is making and what would verify it. Accuracy beats approval.

Label the basis

Observed, sourced, computed, inferred, framed, or unknown/guess.

Do not pretend certainty

If the basis is weak, say what is missing before using the claim.

Separate frames from reality

Framed means true only inside a model, role-play, assumption, or hypothetical.

44-50 min

Copyright questions are risk questions, not instant yes/no answers.

For this course, students do not need to decide copyright law. They need to recognize when source material, AI-generated output, attribution, and permission are uncertain.

Ask two questions

  • Am I allowed to use this AI tool for the assignment?
  • Do I have permission or rights for any uploaded source material?

Use caution when

  • Uploading books, paid articles, lyrics, images, or private files.
  • Claiming authorship over mostly AI-generated work.
  • Using AI images without disclosure.
  • Reusing recognizable source style or protected content.

50-55 min

Do not use AI ranking as a real decision shortcut.

Ranking classmates, applicants, patients, employees, or scholarship candidates is high-stakes. In this course, use those examples only as fairness critiques with fictional or approved data.

Affected people

Who could lose opportunity, privacy, credit, or dignity because of the output?

Proxy variables

Could zip code, school, writing style, name, or activity history stand in for protected or sensitive traits?

Review

What human review, appeal, or evidence would be required before using the output?

Academic integrity

Disclose assistance. Do not outsource authorship.

Cornell and Vanderbilt give useful teaching examples, but your instructor's assignment policy controls. If the policy is unclear, ask before using AI.

Usually defensible

Brainstorming, outlining, grammar checks, critique, debugging help, or source leads when allowed, checked, and disclosed as required.

Usually not defensible

Submitting AI-written work as your own, using hidden AI help when prohibited, inventing sources, or using someone else's data/content without permission.
