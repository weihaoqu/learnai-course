Agent use rules

Agents are allowed only inside clear boundaries.

The ethical question is not "Can the AI do it?" The question is whether the workflow protects people, evidence, authorship, rights, and accountability.

Usually OK if policy permits

  • Brainstorming topics, questions, or examples.
  • Explaining concepts and comparing approaches.
  • Outlining, critique, grammar help on your own work.
  • Code hints, test ideas, organization, and source leads.

Use only with review

  • Drafting, rewriting, summarizing, coding, or data analysis.
  • Image, video, voice, chart, or presentation generation.
  • Email, calendar, file-editing, or database tool actions.
  • Any work where assignment or institution rules vary.

Do not use agents for this

  • Hidden authorship or submitting work you cannot explain.
  • Private, peer, restricted, paid, or copyrighted uploads without permission.
  • Fake citations, fake evidence, impersonation, or deception.
  • High-stakes decisions or actions without explicit human approval.

Memory boundary

Memory is not a transcript. Before asking an assistant to remember anything, ask what will be remembered, why it is needed, where it is stored, who can review or delete it, and whether the information belongs to someone else.

source leads are not evidence; agent output is not permission
Pacing: 4 minutes. Emphasize that "OK" still depends on course policy and disclosure. High-stakes topics can be used for learning explanations, not decisions or recommendations about real people.
8-15 min

Use five questions before using AI.

This simplified model is informed by risk-management thinking: map the task, identify who could be affected, measure what needs evidence, then manage the action with review.

What is the task?

Name the real job: brainstorm, edit, summarize, generate, rank, decide, cite, code, or submit. Different jobs carry different responsibilities.

Example: "Use AI to brainstorm three possible project topics" is different from "Use AI to write my final reflection."
Pacing: 7 minutes. Keep this as a reusable checklist. Students will apply it repeatedly in scenarios.
Accountability

Sometimes the ethical answer is: stop and ask.

Students should not handle every unclear AI situation alone. Escalation is part of responsible use, not a failure.

Private people

Ask before using peer work, personal stories, identifiable images, grades, health details, or family information.

Deception risk

Ask before publishing media that could be mistaken for a real event, real evidence, or a real person.

High stakes

Do not use AI alone for decisions about grades, jobs, scholarships, discipline, health, money, or safety.

Unclear rights

Ask before uploading paid, restricted, copyrighted, workplace, or client material to a third-party tool.

unclear policy + real people or restricted sources = stop and ask
Pacing: 3 minutes. Name likely escalation routes: instructor, supervisor, source owner, affected person, parent/guardian for minors, or approved campus policy channel.
Decision labels

Classify the action, then explain why.

Usually allowed if policy permits

Appropriate support when the student remains responsible, checks the output, and discloses use if required.

Ask first or revise

The idea might be acceptable, but data, source material, tool choice, or assignment rules need clarification.

Not allowed

The action misrepresents authorship, exposes private data, invents evidence, or uses AI for an unauthorized decision.

allowed is conditional: policy + permission + disclosure + verification + accountability
Permission ladder

The more the agent can change the world, the more permission it needs.

Use this ladder when an AI agent can search, draft, click, send, buy, delete, post, invite, edit, or update records.

1. Read-only thinking help

Ask for explanations, examples, outlines, critique, study help, or possible search terms. Do not paste private data.

2. Draft or generate

Use only if allowed. Verify facts, sources, code, and media labels. Disclose AI help when required.

3. Act in the world

Require explicit approval before sending messages, calendar invites, posts, purchases, file changes, shares, deletions, or record updates.

4. High-stakes or unclear

Stop and ask before using AI for real decisions about grades, jobs, money, health, discipline, safety, or opportunities.

explain = lower risk; generate = verify; act = approve; high-stakes = stop
Pacing: 4 minutes. Distinguish "explain a medical/legal/financial concept for learning" from "recommend a real decision." The latter is outside the student workflow.

Privacy default

Do not paste identifiable private or peer data into AI by default.

Red examples

  • A classmate's draft with their name.
  • A screenshot of grades or feedback.
  • A personal email, medical detail, or family issue.
  • A private document from work or an internship.

Safer alternatives

  • Use invented or anonymized examples.
  • Ask the instructor which tool is approved.
  • Describe the problem without identifiers.
  • Get permission before using someone else's content.
private data + no permission + third-party AI = stop

Media ethics

AI images, video, and voice change the trust problem.

Generated media can be useful for design and explanation, but it can also impersonate people, fabricate evidence, or make fiction look like documentation.

Label generated media

Say when an image, voice, video, chart, or scene was AI-generated or AI-edited.

Do not impersonate

Do not create a real person's voice, face, endorsement, confession, or message without explicit permission.

Do not fake evidence

Do not present generated screenshots, photos, documents, or recordings as proof that something happened.

Preserve the source trail

Keep prompts, sources, and edit notes when media is used for school, public, or portfolio work.

if media could be mistaken for real evidence, label it or do not use it
Pacing: 3 minutes. Connect this forward to Week 6 media generation. The point is not to scare students away from creative media; it is to separate design from deception.
Micro-lab

Claim calibration: say how you know.

When an AI gives an answer, identify what kind of claim each sentence is making and what would verify it. Accuracy beats approval.

Label the basis

Observed, sourced, computed, inferred, framed, or unknown/guess.

Do not pretend certainty

If the basis is weak, say what is missing before using the claim.

Separate frames from reality

Framed means true only inside a model, role-play, assumption, or hypothetical.

44-50 min

Copyright questions are risk questions, not instant yes/no answers.

For this course, students do not need to decide copyright law. They need to recognize when source material, AI-generated output, attribution, and permission are uncertain.

Ask two questions

  • Am I allowed to use this AI tool for the assignment?
  • Do I have permission or rights for any uploaded source material?

Use caution when

  • Uploading books, paid articles, lyrics, images, or private files.
  • Claiming authorship over mostly AI-generated work.
  • Using AI images without disclosure.
  • Reusing protected source material, restricted likenesses, or assignment-prohibited imitation.

50-55 min

Do not use AI ranking as a real decision shortcut.

Ranking classmates, applicants, patients, employees, or scholarship candidates is high-stakes. In this course, use those examples only as fairness critiques with fictional or approved data.

Affected people

Who could lose opportunity, privacy, credit, or dignity because of the output?

Proxy variables

Could zip code, school, writing style, name, or activity history stand in for protected or sensitive traits?

Review

What human review, appeal, or evidence would be required before using the output?

Optional depth - 4 min

Bias mitigation is a workflow, not a magic switch.

AI systems can reflect patterns from their training data. Model providers use techniques such as fine-tuning and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) to reduce harmful output, but students still need review habits.

Look for inherited patterns

Test examples where names, roles, writing style, language, or background could change the output unfairly.

Use human feedback carefully

Prefer outputs that are useful, honest, respectful, and evidence-based; reject stereotypes, slurs, harassment, or unsupported claims.

Keep high-stakes limits

Even safer models should not make real decisions about people without policy, evidence, review, appeal, and accountability.

safer model + unchecked workflow = still your responsibility

Beyond correctness

Ethics also includes access, dependence, and hidden costs.

A technically correct AI output can still create unfair pressure, exclude students, or hide costs that should be part of the decision.

Access

Do not design group work that requires paid tools, private accounts, or high-end devices unless an equal alternative exists.

Equity

AI can help with language, disability access, and tutoring, but outputs should not penalize students for accent, style, or background.

Dependence

Use AI as a tool, not as an authority, therapist, friend, or substitute for human responsibility.

Hidden costs

Consider energy use, data work, moderation labor, and whether the AI step has a real learning or work benefit.

a fair workflow gives people a meaningful way to participate without hidden pressure
Pacing: 3 minutes. Keep this practical. Ask students to name one course situation where AI access could be unequal and one accommodation or alternative.
Academic integrity

Disclose assistance. Do not outsource authorship.

Cornell and Vanderbilt give useful teaching examples, but your instructor's assignment policy controls. If the policy is unclear, ask before using AI.

Usually defensible

Brainstorming, outlining, grammar checks, critique, debugging help, or source leads when allowed, checked, and disclosed as required.

Usually not defensible

Submitting AI-written work as your own, using hidden AI help when prohibited, inventing sources, or using someone else's data/content without permission.
