Week 11: Knowledge Acquisition and Career Evidence Studio
Slide 1
60-minute studio

Turn AI-assisted work into credible evidence.

Week 10 gave students a deployed or deployment-ready project. Week 11 teaches them to verify one claim, document AI use, and explain their human decisions in a career-ready way.

In class

Practice lateral reading, claim labeling, AI-use disclosure, and project-story writing.

Outcome

A Week 11 Evidence and Career Packet connected to the student's project.

Boundary

No personal job applications, private resumes, sensitive identity details, or unchecked AI claims.

Pacing: 4 minutes. Frame the week as evidence literacy, not generic resume polish.
4-8 min

The studio path has five moves.

Students move from a public artifact to a verified claim and a career explanation.

Project artifact

Start with the Week 10 app link or fallback file.

AI research

Use AI to propose questions, not final authority.

Lateral reading

Check source, author, organization, and independent support.

Evidence map

Connect action, artifact, skill, and reflection.

Project story

Explain human judgment, not just tool output.

The main mistake

Students may treat fluent AI text as proof. This week separates useful AI help from verified evidence.

8-13 min

AI research is a starting point, not the source of truth.

Students can use Claude Code, Codex, ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, or another assistant to organize questions. They still need source trails for factual claims.

Good AI jobs

  • Suggest search terms and source types.
  • Summarize pasted source text.
  • Compare claims when sources are provided.
  • Draft a disclosure statement.

Not allowed as proof

  • AI-generated citations that were not checked.
  • Confident claims with no source trail.
  • Invented project outcomes or user impact.
  • Private personal details used in class artifacts.
Ask students to name one fact about their project that AI can help investigate but cannot decide alone.
Research mode choice

Choose the right information path.

Power users do not ask every question the same way. They decide whether the answer can come from general model knowledge, current web sources, or a deeper source comparison.

  • Pretrained knowledge: common definitions, stable background, and low-risk summaries.
  • Web search: current events, local options, dates, prices, ratings, or niche sources.
  • Deep research: many sources, competing views, pros and cons, or an evidence report.
  • Student rule: the mode is not proof. Inspect original pages, dates, source type, and claim support.

Source concept: instructor-provided transcript on AI search and research modes. Rewritten for classroom use.

Keep this vendor-neutral. If a tool does not have web search or deep research, students can still compare ordinary search results and source-rich prompting.
13-23 min

Lateral reading means leaving the page.

Before students trust a source, they investigate who is behind it and compare it against independent evidence.

1

Leave

Open new tabs instead of staying inside the original page.

2

Investigate

Check the author, organization, purpose, date, and expertise.

3

Compare

Find at least one independent source that supports, complicates, or contradicts the claim.

4

Return

Decide what can safely be claimed and what remains uncertain.

Use this as a live demo with a non-sensitive topic. The key move is opening outside sources before judging the original page.
23-30 min

Choose claim types students can actually verify.

A career story is stronger when it names evidence honestly instead of overstating impact.

Domain claim

Background about the topic or field the app addresses.

User need claim

A claim about what a user group needs, prefers, or struggles with.

Capability claim

What the app, AI model, or web technology can and cannot do.

Observed project claim

Something students directly tested in their own static app.

Keep the examples modest. Directly observed claims are often the most defensible student evidence.
30-40 min

Build a research log for one project claim.

The log makes the student's source trail visible and separates claim strength from writing style.

40-46 min

Citation and AI disclosure are different jobs.

Cite source material. Disclose AI assistance. Do not cite unchecked AI output as factual authority.

Citation
Used when a claim, fact, quote, dataset, framework, or source idea comes from a human-authored or institutional source.
AI disclosure
Used when AI helped brainstorm, summarize pasted text, draft, revise, code, debug, roleplay, or organize the work.
Not enough
"AI said it" is not a source trail for a factual claim.

Good disclosure

I used an AI assistant to generate search terms, summarize pasted sources, and revise my project story. I checked factual claims against listed sources and changed unsupported language.

Weak disclosure

I used AI for everything, and it gave me the facts.

Purdue Libraries: Citing AI
Keep the rule simple: sources support facts; disclosure describes process.
Optional method

Video sources need transcript plus screen evidence.

A title, thumbnail, or partial transcript is not enough. When a project claim depends on a video, record what is said, what appears on screen, and the timestamp.

1

Permission

Use videos permitted by license/platform terms or instructor-approved sources only. Do not download private, paywalled, classmate, or restricted content.

2

Transcript

Capture the spoken claim with timestamp. Treat auto-transcripts as draft evidence.

3

Frames

Check what is actually visible on screen at the relevant moment.

4

Verify

Compare against another source before turning video content into a project claim.

Career lens - 6 minutes

Jobs are bundles of tasks.

Do not ask only, "Will AI replace this job?" Ask which tasks AI can help, which tasks need human judgment, and which tasks are outside the project boundary.

Student activity

  • Pick one role connected to your project or interests.
  • List 4-6 tasks that role does.
  • Label each task: AI can help, AI should not decide, or not enough evidence.
  • Justify one label using technical feasibility, value, and human risk.

Useful labels

  • Augment: AI drafts, summarizes, organizes, or checks while a human decides.
  • Automate narrow task: low-risk, bounded, tested, and easy to reverse.
  • Do not automate: private, high-stakes, interpersonal, physical, or poorly defined work.

Career reality check

One task that AI can help with is not the whole job. A role can also include communication, documentation, tool operation, exceptions, and accountability.

Use examples like medical imaging as task analysis practice, not as a prediction that a whole profession disappears.

O*NET task lookup DeepLearning.AI: Generative AI for Everyone

Source concepts: DeepLearning.AI, Generative AI for Everyone; O*NET Online; Brynjolfsson, Mitchell, and Rock, What Can Machines Learn?; and Eloundou et al., GPTs are GPTs. Rewritten for classroom use.

Macro studies and job-anxiety examples can start the conversation, but do not turn exposure estimates into predictions about a student's future. Use "task exposure" or "workload exposure," not "job replacement." One automatable task is not the whole role.
After task lens

Map the project to a career-ready skill.

Students should show what they did, where the evidence is, what skill it demonstrates, and what judgment they used.

Final writing move

Write a project story that does not overclaim.

The story should connect purpose, action, evidence, and human review in one paragraph.

This is not a final resume. It is a classroom-safe project story students can later adapt.
Optional extension

Use roleplay to practice explaining judgment.

The agent can ask interview-style questions, but students should use fictional or classroom-safe details.

Safe roleplay prompt

Act as a friendly interviewer for a student project showcase. Ask me five questions about my static web app, my evidence trail, and my human review decisions. Do not ask for private personal information. After each answer, give one specific improvement suggestion.

Privacy boundary

  • No home addresses, phone numbers, school IDs, health, immigration, financial, or family details.
  • No real job applications during class unless the teacher explicitly approves the context.
  • Students may use a fictional role, company, or audience.
This can become a pair activity if accounts or agents are unavailable.
